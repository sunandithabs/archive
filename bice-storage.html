BICEStorage Investigation

A routine filesystem check that turned up a research project quietly outgrowing its own footprint.

Overview

A storage audit was run as routine maintenance, not in response to any pressure on disk space. The goal was simply to understand where space was going and whether anything could be reclaimed. It turned into a short investigation into how a single research project, BICE, had accumulated a disproportionate share of total usage.

Discovery

A top-level home directory scan showed one project directory standing far above everything else. Drilling into it pointed at a single subproject as the dominant consumer, with the rest of the workspace contributing comparatively little. The imbalance was the signal worth following, not the absolute numbers.

Analysis

Inside the subproject, most of the space was split between a generated database file, a research dataset directory, and a compressed archive. The dataset itself consisted of labeled network traffic captures used for IoT malware analysis, including botnet attack traffic and benign baseline traffic, the kind of files that grow large quickly and tend to get copied rather than referenced.

A closer look at related directories elsewhere on the system showed the same dataset files duplicated across multiple archive folders. Different extraction passes had each left their own full copy behind instead of being cleaned up afterward.

Cleanup Strategy

Rather than deleting anything immediately, the approach was to verify before removing. Folder sizes were compared, and duplicate archive directories were diffed against each other to confirm they held identical content before any of them were cleared. Once confirmed, redundant copies were removed and the remaining dataset was moved into a dedicated research data location instead of living alongside temporary downloads. Routine cache and package cleanup was run alongside this work and recovered a modest amount of additional space on its own.

Key Takeaway

The system was never at risk. What this audit actually found was a dataset management problem dressed up as a storage problem: large research files copied across multiple working folders with no single owner keeping track of which copy was current.

The fix was procedural, not technical. Verify before deleting, give datasets one canonical location instead of letting them spread, and treat duplication as the default outcome of repeated extraction unless something actively prevents it.
