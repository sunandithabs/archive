BICEBehavioral Identity Continuity Engine

Research Paper ICOSICS 2026

Overview

BICE watches an IoT device's own behavior over time instead of comparing it to everyone else's. It builds a running baseline per device, three behavioral angles, DNS pattern, who it talks to, how it uses ports, and flags the device when it drifts away from its own normal. No labeled attack traffic needed to train it, which is the part I actually care about.

This is the summary version. The actual paper has the equations, the full evaluation tables, and the citation list a reviewer needs. This page exists so someone can understand what the project is without reading a submission that's currently sitting in someone else's review queue.

Research Question

Most IoT intrusion detection looks at one moment at a time. A packet either trips a rule or it doesn't. That works fine for loud attacks. It does nothing for an attack that moves slowly enough to stay under every threshold, a device whose DNS pattern drifts a little more each day instead of changing all at once.

So the question: can a detector learn what "normal" looks like for one specific device, and catch drift away from that baseline over time, without needing labeled attack data to train on in the first place?

Why I Started This

Got annoyed reading IoT security papers that train one population-level model and apply it to every device on the network, smart bulb and security camera alike, like they should behave the same. They don't. A thermostat has a boring, predictable life. A camera doesn't. Scoring both against one shared average felt like checking everyone's temperature against the same baseline and wondering why the readings make no sense.

So I went per-device instead. Mostly to see if it would hold up before I cared about whether it was a clever idea.

Methodology

Each device gets its own baseline built from normal operation, no attack traffic involved. Three features feed it: DNS query entropy, how many distinct destinations it contacts, and its port access pattern. Stats update online using Welford's method, so nothing has to be stored and nothing gets replayed from history.

Every tick, the device's current behavior gets scored against its own baseline. When the combined drift crosses a threshold, the device gets flagged and quarantined pending a human looking at it. Baseline updates freeze the moment an alert fires, so a slow attacker can't just drag the baseline along with them and erase their own trail.

That's the shape of it. The actual scoring math, the aggregation choice, and the calibration process are in the paper, not here.

Key Findings

Evaluated on the N-BaIoT benchmark, 89 device-attack profiles across 9 physical devices. Caught all 80 attack profiles, no false alarms across the 9 benign ones. That number is real but it's also suspiciously clean, and getting there involved actual bugs, not just good design.

the bugs that almost ruined it

Early pilot run, the model looked perfect: 0% false positives. Turned out a label bug had quietly reset every device to "benign" on every tick, so nothing was actually being checked. A perfect score for entirely the wrong reason, which is a special kind of embarrassing to catch yourself doing. Found a couple other harness gremlins after that, a sleep call throttling tick rate to uselessness, a tilde in a file path that silently failed to expand. None of them were the detection model's fault, but every single one would have made the final numbers meaningless if they'd shipped unnoticed.

Current Status

Submitted to ICOSICS 2026, sitting in review as of writing. Final audit pass and citation corrections are done on my end. At this point it's mostly waiting and trying not to refresh the email too often.

Limitations

One dataset, two malware families. No test yet against an attacker who knows the detector exists and deliberately mimics normal behavior to slip under it. The threshold and the trust-score curve were tuned against the same data they're evaluated on, no separate held-out set, so I'd want to see this hold up on traffic it's never touched before I'd call it proven. Long-running real-world deployment, firmware updates, seasonal usage drift, none of that has been tested.

Future Work

Real deployment testing on actual network traffic, head-to-head comparison against other unsupervised per-device baselines under identical conditions, and eventually a kernel-resident version that doesn't need a dedicated monitoring host watching from the outside.
