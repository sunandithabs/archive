EXCALIBURA Lifecycle Threat Model for Adaptive CAN Intrusion Detection

Research Paper 2026 Read the Paper (DOI) ↗

Overview

Adaptive CAN intrusion detectors keep learning after they ship, which means they all have a calibration window somewhere, a moment where the detector is still deciding what "normal" traffic looks like. Nobody had asked what happens if an attacker gets bus access during exactly that window. EXCALIBUR names that window as an attacker-reachable surface and backs it with a case study: a bounded-budget timestamp-drift attack against a minimal EWMA/Z-score CAN IDS, run on the real ORNL ROAD dataset across three CAN IDs, four ambient drive sessions, and a seven-point attacker-budget sweep.

The headline result is a clean one. Baseline shift tracks attacker budget almost exactly, a 0.0050 ratio holding across all 70 tested points, with one deviation three orders of magnitude smaller than the shift itself. What's messier is whether the attack is visible to a defender watching flag rates, and that's where the paper spends most of its honesty.

Jump to Status

Vehicle software update cadence is climbing toward continuous OTA delivery, and a learning component that gets initialized or reset alongside an update is turning into a routine event instead of a one-time factory step. That's motivation, not proof, nobody has shown adaptive CAN IDS recalibrate on any fixed schedule in a production vehicle. The question EXCALIBUR actually asks is narrower: if an attacker had bounded CAN bus access during a calibration window, could they shift what the detector learns as normal, and would that shift survive long enough to matter?

The adversary gets CAN bus access during a single calibration window and can perturb a bounded fraction, the "budget", of frames belonging to one target CAN ID within that window only. Everything outside the window, and every other ID, stays untouched. Granting that capability is standard threat-modeling practice, it lets the paper measure what the capability would achieve, not claim it's achievable against a production vehicle today.

Explicitly out of scope: ECU functional, sensor, or UDS/diagnostic calibration, physical ECU compromise, tampering with the monitoring point itself, and any claim that adaptive CAN IDS are currently deployed in production vehicles.

Reference IDS is deliberately minimal: a per-ID EWMA-updated mean/std with Z-score flagging (α = 0.1, threshold 3.0). It's not meant to be a strong detector, its job is to make the calibration window measurable. The attack adds a fixed per-frame timestamp drift to a bounded fraction of a target ID's frames inside the calibration window, budget swept from 0.01 to 0.5 at a fixed drift, drift separately swept from 0.001s to 0.020s at fixed budget.

Evaluated on the real ORNL ROAD dataset, three CAN IDs (125, 354, 193) across four ambient drive sessions, 70 budget-sweep points total. Two post-calibration windows get reported throughout: an early window (first 200 scored frames) where the attack's effect shows at full strength, and a full window where EWMA adaptation has time to absorb the drift.

Baseline shift is the cleanest finding. It scales linearly with both attacker budget and drift magnitude, shift/budget holds at 0.0050 across all 70 (session, target, budget) points, and a mixed-effects model pooled across every point agrees, with only one session showing a detectable deviation, roughly three orders of magnitude smaller than the shift values it sits next to.

Early-window flag suppression is conditional, not universal. Wherever clean-traffic flagging isn't already near the 200-frame measurement floor, the attack suppresses the flag count monotonically as budget rises. Where clean traffic already sits at that floor, the confidence intervals on clean vs. attacked counts overlap almost completely, so the paper reports those pairs as unresolved rather than claiming an effect either way. Full-window flag rates stay close between clean and attacked traffic everywhere, consistent with EWMA adaptation absorbing the calibration-time shift over time.

RobustIDS bundles three changes together: swaps the EWMA/Z-score estimator for median/MAD, caps how far any single tick can move the baseline, and raises the alert threshold from 3.0 to 5.0 with a variance floor so the MAD denominator can't collapse on low-jitter signals. On target 125's basic_long session, its full-window flag rate under attack (0.0019) sits well below the clean EWMA baseline (0.0266).

The three changes were evaluated as a bundle, not in isolation, so the paper is careful not to claim which one is doing the work. Under attack, RobustIDS's early-window count rises to a flat 1/200 across nearly every budget and session, roughly one additional flagged frame out of 200, and that uptick does not scale with attacker budget the way the raw baseline shift does.

The attacker-access assumption and the calibration/post-calibration boundary are both threat-modeling premises, not evidence anyone has achieved either against a real vehicle. RobustIDS's mitigation effect is bundled across three design changes and doesn't respond to attacker budget, so it can't yet be separated from a floor artifact. Early-window counts are small (0–16 out of 200), and three of the ten evaluated pairs start at floor-level clean flagging, where this study's design genuinely can't distinguish a small real effect from no effect.

Coverage is uneven too: IDs 125 and 354 are absent from the extended_long session, so only ID 193 has full four-session coverage, and the significance test and estimator comparison both draw on a single session/target pair. Two measurement bugs were caught and fixed during implementation, both from reading detector state at the wrong point in its update cycle, documented in full in the paper rather than glossed over here.

Case study complete on real CAN traffic, threat model and evaluation written up. This is a threat-modeling exercise on real data, not a demonstrated production attack, and the paper is explicit about that distinction throughout.
